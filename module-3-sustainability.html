Module 03 Theory Core Concepts

Views of Sustainability: Weak, Strong & Deep Green

Three positions on the nature–economy relationship, Haughton's five equity principles, and three approaches to sustainability action.

By the end

Distinguish weak vs strong vs deep green sustainability and their policy implications.

Apply

Haughton's 5 equity principles (futurity, social justice, trans-frontier, procedural, interspecies).

Evaluate

Status quo vs reform vs transformation approaches in real cases.

01

Weak vs Strong vs Deep Green

Three positions on substitutability of natural capital

Weak sustainability: Natural and manufactured capital are substitutable; technology fills gaps (e.g., air purifiers).
Strong sustainability: Critical natural capital (ozone, climate) is NOT substitutable; functions are irreplaceable.
Deep green: Non-human species, natural systems, biodiversity have intrinsic rights and values.
Analogy: A factory pollutes air. Weak: install air purifiers (tech fix). Strong: stop polluting because atmosphere's cleansing capacity is finite and irreplaceable. Deep green: the ecosystem has right to exist unpolluted regardless of human utility.

Figure 1: Spectrum from weak (substitutable) to deep green (intrinsic rights)

Worked Example: Climate Policy

  1. Weak: Carbon capture tech allows continued fossil fuel use; GDP growth continues
  2. Strong: Phase out fossil fuels; atmospheric CO₂ capacity is finite and non-substitutable
  3. Deep Green: Recognize rights of future generations and non-human species to stable climate
Exam sentence: "Weak sustainability trusts technology to substitute nature; strong sustainability draws red lines at critical natural capital; deep green grants nature standing in its own right."
Trap: Confusing "strong sustainability" with "deep ecology." Strong sustainability still anthropocentric (human needs); deep green is ecocentric (nature for itself).
60-second teach-back: Explain the spectrum with one policy example

Weak: Capital substitutable → tech fixes OK (e.g., CCS for coal)
Strong: Critical natural capital non-substitutable → red lines (e.g., 1.5°C limit)
Deep Green: Nature has intrinsic rights → legal standing for rivers/ecosystems

02

Haughton's Five Principles of Equity

Operationalizing intergenerational and intragenerational justice

Futurity: Inter-generational equity — future generations' needs equal to present.
Social Justice: Intra-generational equity — fair distribution within present.
Trans-frontier Responsibility: Geographical equity — cross-border impacts.
Procedural Equity: Open, fair, participatory decision-making.
Interspecies Equity: Biodiversity has value beyond human use.
Analogy: A family trust fund. Futurity = preserve principal for grandchildren. Social Justice = all siblings get fair share now. Trans-frontier = don't pollute neighbor's yard. Procedural = all family members vote on withdrawals. Interspecies = the family dog gets care too.

Figure 2: Haughton's five equity principles

Worked Example: Transboundary River

  1. Futurity: Don't deplete aquifer for current irrigation
  2. Social Justice: Upstream/downstream users share water equitably
  3. Trans-frontier: Country A doesn't divert river harming Country B
  4. Procedural: All riparian communities participate in water treaty
  5. Interspecies: Environmental flows maintained for fish/migration
Exam sentence: "Sustainability without equity is just resource management for the privileged; Haughton's five principles make justice operational across time, space, process, and species."
Trap: "Futurity" ≠ "future discounting." Futurity says future needs = present needs (no discounting). Standard economics discounts future; sustainability ethics rejects this.
60-second teach-back: Name all 5 principles with one-line each
  1. Futurity = intergenerational equity (future = present)
  2. Social Justice = intragenerational equity (fairness now)
  3. Trans-frontier = geographical equity (cross-border)
  4. Procedural = fair process (participation, transparency)
  5. Interspecies = biodiversity equity (nature's share)
03

Three Approaches to Sustainability

Status quo, reform, or transformation?

Status Quo: Adjustments without fundamental change; economic growth = solution.
Reform: Root problem = imbalances/lack of knowledge; solution = policy + technology.
Transformation: Root problem = fundamental society–nature relations; solution = inclusive social/political action.
Analogy: A leaking roof. Status quo: put buckets under leaks. Reform: fix roof with better materials. Transformation: redesign house for climate resilience and rethink why we build in floodplains.

Figure 3: Status quo → Reform → Transformation (increasing depth of change)

Worked Example: Plastic Pollution

  1. Status Quo: Better recycling, biodegradable plastics, consumer education
  2. Reform: Extended producer responsibility, plastic bans, circular economy policies
  3. Transformation: Redesign production/consumption systems; eliminate single-use culture; redefine progress
Exam sentence: "Status quo manages symptoms; reform fixes mechanisms; transformation changes the system that produces the problem."
Trap: "Reform" sounds like change but keeps the system intact. True transformation questions why the system produces the problem in the first place.
60-second teach-back: Contrast the three with one issue

Status Quo: Manage symptoms, growth = solution
Reform: Fix imbalances, policy + tech
Transformation: Change root society–nature relations, inclusive action
Example (plastic): Recycling → bans/EPR → zero-waste circular systems

Module 3 Practice MCQs

M3-1. A government promotes carbon capture and storage (CCS) technology as the primary solution to meet net-zero targets while continuing coal power. This reflects:

✓ Correct Answer: Weak sustainability

M3-2. Which of Haughton's principles is violated when a country exports hazardous waste to a poorer nation with weaker regulations?

✓ Correct Answer: Trans-frontier Responsibility

M3-3. A city bans single-use plastic bags and promotes reusable alternatives. This is an example of:

✓ Correct Answer: Reform approach

M3-4. "Future generations have the same right to a stable climate as we do." This statement embodies which Haughton principle?

✓ Correct Answer: Futurity

M3-5. A community facing industrial pollution organizes, demands systemic change in production systems, and links their struggle to global climate justice movements. This is:

✓ Correct Answer: Transformation

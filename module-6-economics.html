Environmental Economics: I=P×A×T, Externalities & Policy

The economic framework for environmental problems: IPAT identity, public goods, externalities, Coase theorem, and policy instruments.

By the end

Apply I=P×A×T to any environmental problem and identify leverage points.

Explain

Why environmental problems occur: public goods, tragedy of commons, prisoner's dilemma.

Classify

Externalities: positive/negative, depletable/non-depletable; private vs social costs.

Evaluate

Policy instruments: CAC vs market-based (taxes, permits, subsidies); Coase theorem.

01

I = P × A × T: The Environmental Impact Identity

Decomposing environmental impact into three multiplicative factors

I (Impact) = P (Population) × A (Affluence/Consumption per person) × T (Technology/Impact per unit consumption)
All three factors multiply — no single factor is sufficient alone. Reducing any factor reduces total impact.
Analogy: Household waste = (Number of people) × (Consumption per person) × (Packaging per unit). You can reduce waste by fewer people, less consumption, or less packaging — but all three matter.

Figure 1: IPAT identity — three multiplicative drivers of environmental impact

Worked Example: Urban Air Pollution

  1. P: City population growing 2%/year → more vehicles, more energy demand
  2. A: Rising income → more cars per household, more AC, more consumption
  3. T: Vehicle efficiency improving (lower T), but Jevons paradox: efficiency → more driving
  4. Policy: Target all three: public transit (↓A), emission standards (↓T), urban planning (↓P pressure)
Exam sentence: "IPAT is not a prediction — it's an accounting identity. Any environmental policy must address at least one factor; ignoring any factor guarantees failure."
Trap: "Technology will save us" (T↓ solves everything). Jevons Paradox: efficiency gains often increase total consumption. Also, T can be >1 (dirty tech) or <1 (clean tech) — not automatically good.
60-second teach-back: IPAT factors with one policy lever each
  1. P = Population → family planning, women's education, urban planning
  2. A = Affluence/Consumption → carbon tax, circular economy, sufficiency
  3. T = Technology/Impact per unit → efficiency standards, clean tech, BAT
02

Why Environmental Problems Occur

Public goods, tragedy of commons, prisoner's dilemma, externalities

Public goods: Non-excludable + non-rival (clean air) OR rival (fisheries = tragedy of commons).
Tragedy of commons: Open access + self-interest > social interest → overexploitation.
Prisoner's dilemma: Individual rationality → collective irrationality (both confess).
Externalities: Uncompensated interdependence — private cost ≠ social cost.
Analogy: A village pasture. Each herder adds one more cow (private benefit), but the cost (grass depletion) is shared. Individually rational → collectively ruinous. This is the tragedy of commons = prisoner's dilemma = negative externality.

Pure public goodNon-excludable, non-rivalGovernment provision

Exam sentence: "Environmental problems are market failures: missing prices for nature's services, missing property rights for commons, and missing institutions for collective action."
Trap: "Tragedy of commons" ≠ "commons are doomed." Ostrom showed communities CAN self-govern commons with design principles (clear boundaries, congruence, collective choice, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, minimal rights, nested enterprises).
60-second teach-back: Why do env problems occur? (3 reasons)
  1. Public goods → no price, no exclusion → under-provision/overuse
  2. Externalities → private cost ≠ social cost → market failure
  3. Prisoner's dilemma → individual rationality → collective ruin
02b

Goods Classification: Excludability & Subtractibility

The 2×2 matrix defining public goods, commons, club goods, private goods

Two key properties:
  1. Excludability: Can people be prevented from using the good? (Yes/No)
  2. Subtractibility (Rivalry): Does one person's use reduce availability for others? (Yes/No)
Subtractible = Rival. Non-subtractible = Non-rival.
Analogy: A cinema seat (excludable, subtractible), a public park (non-excludable, subtractible), cable TV (excludable, non-subtractible), clean air (non-excludable, non-subtractible).

Worked Example: Classify These Goods

  1. City park (unfenced): Non-excludable, subtractible → CPR (congestion)
  2. City park (fenced, fee): Excludable, subtractible → Private/Club good
  3. National defense: Non-excludable, non-subtractible → Pure public good
  4. Cable TV: Excludable, non-subtractible → Club good
  5. Fishery (open access): Non-excludable, subtractible → CPR → Tragedy
  6. Knowledge (published): Non-excludable, non-subtractible → Public good
Exam sentence: "Excludability + Subtractibility = 4 types of goods. Environmental problems cluster in CPR (non-excludable, subtractible) and pure public goods (non-excludable, non-subtractible) — both suffer from missing markets."
Trap: "Public good" ≠ "government provided." Public good = non-excludable + non-subtractible. Many government services are NOT public goods (e.g., healthcare is excludable). Also: "subtractible" = "rival" = "rivalrous" — same concept, different words.

03

Externalities: Types, Costs & Classification

Private vs social costs, positive/negative, depletable/non-depletable

Externality: Unintended, uncompensated side effect of one's activity on another.
Private cost: Direct cost to producer (fuel, maintenance).
Social cost: Private cost + external cost (pollution health effects).
Negative externality: Social cost > Private cost (pollution).
Positive externality: Private cost > Social cost (R&D, vaccination, reforestation).
Depletable: Use by one reduces availability for others (manure for fuel vs fertilizer).
Non-depletable: Use by one doesn't reduce for others (air pollution, knowledge).
Analogy: A factory pollutes river. Private cost = treatment plant cost. Social cost = treatment + downstream health costs + fishery loss + recreation loss. Negative externality = social > private. If factory pays only private cost, it overproduces.

Figure: Externalities and policy instruments comparison

Worked Example: Vehicle Air Pollution

  1. Private cost: Fuel, maintenance, insurance
  2. External cost: Asthma cases, climate change, noise, congestion
  3. Social cost: Private + External
  4. Negative externality: Social > Private (driver doesn't pay for asthma)
  5. Non-depletable: My pollution doesn't reduce your ability to pollute
  6. Policy: Congestion charge, fuel tax, emission standards → internalize externality
Exam sentence: "An externality exists when social cost diverges from private cost. The policy goal: internalize the externality so the polluter faces the true social cost of their actions."
Trap: "Positive externality" ≠ "good thing, no policy needed." Positive externalities (R&D, education, vaccination) are UNDER-provided by markets because producers can't capture full social benefit → need subsidies/public provision.
60-second teach-back: Classify externalities with examples

Negative: Social cost > Private (pollution, noise, congestion)
Positive: Social benefit > Private (R&D, vaccination, education, reforestation)
Depletable: Rival in externality (manure for fuel vs fertilizer)
Non-depletable: Non-rival externality (air pollution, knowledge, noise)
Key: Externalities = non-market interdependence

04

Policy Instruments: CAC vs Market-Based

Coase theorem, polluter pays, standards vs taxes vs permits

Coase Theorem: If property rights clear + transaction costs low → bargaining achieves efficient outcome regardless of initial allocation.
Polluter Pays Principle: Polluter bears cost of pollution prevention/control.
CAC (Command & Control): Standards (MINAS), bans, permits/quotas/licenses.
Market-based: Taxes (effluent, product, input), tradable permits, subsidies, deposit-refund.
Analogy: CAC = speed limit (command). Tax = toll per km (price). Permit = limited driving passes (quantity). CAC = certain compliance, high cost. Tax = cost certainty, quantity uncertain. Permit = quantity certainty, price varies.

Figure: CAC vs Taxes vs Permits — comparison across 7 criteria

Worked Example: SO₂ Allowance Trading (US Acid Rain Program)

  1. Cap: Total SO₂ allowances = environmental target
  2. Allowance: 1 ton SO₂/year, bankable, tradable
  3. Why worked: Wide cost differences (flexibility), simple rules, enforcement
  4. Result: 50% cost savings vs CAC; rapid innovation in scrubbers
Exam sentence: "No perfect instrument exists. CAC = administrative ease, goal certainty at firm level. Taxes = static/dynamic efficiency, revenue. Permits = aggregate goal certainty, market efficiency IF not too thin."
Trap: "Permits are always better than taxes." Permits fail if market too thin (few traders). Taxes fail if optimal tax level unknown. CAC fails cost efficiency but easiest to administer. Context determines choice.
60-second teach-back: CAC vs Taxes vs Permits — one pro/con each

CAC: Pro = admin ease, firm-level certainty. Con = no cost efficiency, high entry barriers
Taxes: Pro = cost efficiency, revenue. Con = goal uncertainty (optimal tax unknown)
Permits: Pro = aggregate goal certainty, cost efficiency. Con = hard to administer, thin market risk
Polluter Pays: All three can satisfy IF permits auctioned

Module 6 Practice MCQs

M6-1. In the IPAT identity, if population grows 1%, affluence grows 2%, and technology improves (impact/unit drops) 1%, the net environmental impact change is approximately:

✓ Correct Answer: +2%

M6-2. A factory emits pollution harming downstream fishermen. The factory's private cost = ₹100/unit; social cost = ₹150/unit. This is:

✓ Correct Answer: Negative externality

M6-3. Coase Theorem suggests that with well-defined property rights and low transaction costs:

✓ Correct Answer: Bargaining achieves efficient outcome regardless of initial rights allocation

M6-4. Comparing CAC, Taxes, and Tradable Permits for achieving a specific pollution reduction target with certainty:

✓ Correct Answer: Tradable Permits best

M6-5. The US SO₂ allowance trading program succeeded because:

✓ Correct Answer: Wide cost differences + flexible policy + straightforward implementation + enforcement

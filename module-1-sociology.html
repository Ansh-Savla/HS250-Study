Sociology of Environment: Why Care?

Understanding environment–society relationships through social structures, cultural perceptions, and development paradigms. The foundation for all later modules.

By the end

Explain why environmental problems are fundamentally social problems, not just technical ones.

Distinguish

Utilitarian vs intrinsic views of nature and their policy consequences.

Define development

Contrast GNP/GDP per capita with HDI as measures of progress.

Apply

Use the "Why Care?" framework to analyze any environmental issue.

01

Why Care? Five Sociological Reasons

The core framework — environment as social, not just physical

Core idea: Environmental problems are social problems. Technology, perception, response, dependence, and organization are all socially shaped.
Analogy: A forest isn't just trees — it's a cultural symbol, an economic resource, a political battleground, and a life-support system simultaneously. Which lens you use changes what you see and what you do.

Figure 1: The "Why Care?" framework — five sociological lenses on any environmental problem

Worked Example: Applying "Why Care?" to Plastic Pollution

  1. Technology: Plastic invented as cultural response to need for cheap, durable packaging (post-WWII consumer culture)
  2. Perception: Plastic seen as "modern," "hygienic," "convenient" — cultural values shape what we notice (visible litter vs invisible microplastics)
  3. Response: Bans target single-use bags (visible) but not industrial packaging (power of packaging lobby); recycling shifts burden to consumers
  4. Dependence: Food systems, medical supply chains, clothing all depend on plastic — natural life support (oceans) degraded
  5. Organization: Global supply chains organized around plastic logistics; waste colonialism exports pollution to Global South
Case: Indira Gandhi at Stockholm 1972

"Are not poverty and need the greatest polluters?" — Reframed environmental degradation as a problem of inequality, not just pollution. Poverty forces people to overuse commons for survival.

Exam sentence: "Environmental problems are social problems because technology, perception, response, dependence, and organization are all socially structured — there is no 'purely technical' fix."
Trap: Confusing "technology as cause" with "technology as cultural response." Technology doesn't act alone — it's deployed within social values, power structures, and economic systems.
60-second teach-back: Explain the 5 "Why Care?" points to a friend
  1. Technology = cultural response, not neutral tool
  2. Perception = socially constructed, not objective
  3. Response = shaped by power/institutions, not just science
  4. Dependence = societies need nature's life support
  5. Organization = shaped by how we materially intervene in nature
02

What is Nature? Utilitarian vs Intrinsic

Two fundamentally different value frameworks

Utilitarian (Instrumental): Nature as physical entity to be exploited for human use — value = what it provides us.
Non-utilitarian (Intrinsic): Nature as life-support system with inherent worth — value = existence itself, regardless of human use.
Analogy: A river as utilitarian = irrigation water, hydroelectric power, waste disposal. A river as intrinsic = has right to flow, supports ecosystems independent of us, spiritual significance. Same river, two ontologies.

Figure 2: The utilitarian/intrinsic divide shapes every environmental policy debate

Worked Example: Dam on a River

  1. Utilitarian calculus: Electricity (MW), irrigation (hectares), flood control (rupees saved) vs displacement cost, sediment loss
  2. Intrinsic concerns: River's right to flow, fish migration, downstream delta survival, cultural/spiritual loss for riverine communities
  3. Policy result: Cost-benefit analysis (utilitarian) usually wins; intrinsic values appear as "externalities" or "intangibles"
  4. Middle ground: Environmental flows (e-flows) — minimum water for ecological integrity
Exam sentence: "The utilitarian/intrinsic divide is not academic — it determines whether a forest is timber stock or a living community, whether a river is a pipe or a being."
Trap: Thinking intrinsic value means "no human use ever." Intrinsic value means nature has worth independent of human use, not that humans cannot use it at all. Sustainable use respects intrinsic value.
60-second teach-back: Contrast utilitarian vs intrinsic with one example

Utilitarian: Value = usefulness to humans. Forest = timber, carbon credits, recreation revenue.
Intrinsic: Value = existence itself. Forest = has right to exist, supports its own integrity, intergenerational legacy.
Example: Wetland — utilitarian: flood control, water filtration; intrinsic: habitat, biodiversity, cultural heritage.

03

Development: MDGs & Competing Definitions

What counts as progress? GDP vs HDI vs sustainability

Millennium Development Goals (2000): 8 goals — poverty, education, gender, child mortality, maternal health, disease, environment, global partnership.
Development definitions: (1) GNP/GDP per capita — economic output per person. (2) HDI — UN's composite: life expectancy, education, income.
Analogy: GDP per capita is like judging a household by total income — ignores whether kids are educated, parents are healthy, or savings exist for emergencies. HDI adds those dimensions but still misses ecological sustainability.
MeasureWhat it capturesWhat it missesPolicy bias
GNP/GDP per capitaEconomic activity, market outputInequality, unpaid work, environmental depletion, well-beingGrowth at all costs
HDIHealth, education, incomeInequality within countries, ecological footprint, political freedomHuman development, but still anthropocentric
Sustainable DevelopmentPresent + future needs, equity, ecologyHard to quantify, political resistanceLong-term, intergenerational

Figure 3: Nested development measures — each adds dimensions but loses simplicity

Exam sentence: "GDP measures market activity; HDI adds human capabilities; Sustainable Development adds intergenerational equity and ecological limits — each expands the moral circle of what counts as progress."
Trap: Equating "development" with "GDP growth." Many exam questions test whether you recognize HDI and SD as critiques of GDP, not alternatives that can be maximized simultaneously without trade-offs.
60-second teach-back: Rank GDP, HDI, SD by scope and name one blind spot each

1. GDP — narrowest; blind to inequality, ecology, unpaid work
2. HDI — adds health/education; blind to inequality within countries, ecological footprint
3. Sustainable Development — adds future generations, equity, ecology; blind to political feasibility, measurement challenges

Module 1 Practice MCQs

Application questions from notes + past exams

1. According to the "Why Care?" framework, which statement best captures why environmental problems are fundamentally social problems?

✓ Correct Answer: Technology, perception, response, dependence, and social organization are all socially shaped

2. A policymaker argues: "We should protect this wetland because it provides $5M/year in flood control services." This argument reflects which view of nature?

✓ Correct Answer: Utilitarian (instrumental)

3. The Millennium Development Goals (2000) included "Ensure environmental sustainability" as Goal 7. What is a key limitation of the MDG framework from a sustainability perspective?

✓ Correct Answer: Environment was one goal among eight, not integrated across all goals

4. Indira Gandhi's 1972 Stockholm statement — "Are not poverty and need the greatest polluters?" — reframes environmental degradation as primarily a problem of:

✓ Correct Answer: Inequality and survival needs

5. HDI improves on GDP per capita by adding health and education. However, HDI still shares a fundamental blind spot with GDP. What is it?

✓ Correct Answer: Both are anthropocentric and ignore ecological sustainability
